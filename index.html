About

I am a Ph.D. candidate in Political Science at Duke University, where my work sits at the intersection of civil–military relations, security, peace, and conflict, and political behavior and identities.

My dissertation asks how United States (U.S.) military members and veterans evaluate wrongdoing within the ranks — and why judgments about the person who committed the misconduct so often diverge from judgments about the act itself. I combine pre-registered survey experiments with military and veteran samples, comparative case analysis of war crimes, and work on presidential pardons of military members.

I am on the academic job market in the 2026–2027 cycle, with a defense planned for Spring 2027. I hold dual U.S. and Canadian citizenship. Away from research, I am an amateur birder, hold an Amateur Extra Class Ham Radio license, and love cheering for my Michigan sports teams and collecting Criterion Collection films.

Research

My dissertation consists of three papers. The first two are quantitative studies exploring various aspects of a factorial survey experiment, where respondents evaluate a vignette involving a U.S. Army lieutenant directing the shooting of an unarmed civilian motorcyclist. The third paper is qualitative, analyzing several comparative case studies of U.S. military war crimes, the responses from the military, and the lessons learned from each case.

P1 Working paper

Breaking the Rules of Engagement: Moral Judgment and Misconduct in the U.S. Military

A pre-registered 2×2 survey experiment with 553 U.S. military members and veterans. Making a perpetrator personally abrasive shifted how respondents judged them as a person — colder, more deserving of punishment — without moving their approval of the act or their willingness to report it. Moral evaluation of the person and of the act became separate.

Method: factorial vignette experiment · N = 553 · Prolific

P2 In progress

When the Commander-in-Chief Forgives: How Presidential Pardons, Religious Identity, and Social Media Exposure Shape U.S. Military Members' Moral Judgments

Extends person–act dissociation to the politics of presidential pardons for military members, testing partisanship, religious identity, and partisan social-media exposure as moderators of how a pardon reshapes military and public judgment.

Method: survey experiment (pardon subsample) · pre-analysis plan

P3 In progress

One Bad Apple: Socialization, Ethics Training, and the Making of a Violation

Builds a typological theory of when institutional reform follows U.S. military war crimes. Across the My Lai massacre, the Abu Ghraib prison scandal, and the Eddie Gallagher trial and presidential pardon, it traces how three conditions — the adequacy of ethics training, whether the military resists apologist narratives, and political intervention — combine to yield full, partial, or no reform.

Method: typological theory & process tracing · comparative case studies
